# Offer Teardown & Build Kit

Two directions, one lens. Point it at a competitor to see exactly
what their offer is doing, or point it at a blank page to build your
own.

---

## A note on where this comes from

The underlying value theory here is Alex Hormozi's, from *$100M
Offers*. If you have not read it, read it. This document does not
reproduce it and does not try to replace it.

What this kit adds is the part his book does not cover: what happens
when you take a framework built for high-ticket services, coaching,
and info products, and apply it to a physical product with real
COGS, thin margins, shipping costs, and price transparency across
every competitor in your category.

That translation is where most DTC offers fall apart, and it is the
whole point of this document.

---

## Before you start

### Read the log

If you have run this before, read `references/Log.md`. If this is
your first run, note that and continue.

### Confirm the inputs

**To tear down a competitor's offer**, you need the actual offer
page. Screenshot it or paste the copy. A description of the offer
loses the framing layer, which is often where the real work is
happening.

**To build an offer**, do not start until these are confirmed:

1. **The product**, what it is and what a buyer actually receives
2. **Price, landed COGS, and fulfillment cost per order.** Not
   estimates. If bonuses are physical, their fulfillment cost too.
3. **Time to first noticeable result.** A physical fact, not a
   marketing number. Your guarantee window depends on it.
4. **Your real reviews**, at least twenty, including the bad ones
5. **Your CAC ceiling** (see below)
6. **Your current acquisition cost**, blended, for new customers

Ask for anything missing and wait. Do not proceed with placeholders.
An offer built on a guessed margin is an offer you cannot afford to
run, and it will look exactly as convincing as one you can.

### If you do not have a CAC ceiling

You need one. Section 5 is built on it and the whole thing is
theatre without it.

The quick version:

    CAC Ceiling = Net AOV x Gross Margin % x Allowable Marketing %

Use 30% as the allowable marketing percentage unless you have a
reason not to. Net AOV is average order value after discounts and
returns. Gross margin is after COGS *and* fulfillment, not just
COGS.

That gets you a working number in two minutes. A proper version
comes from running your P&L through the four buckets, which will
also tell you whether your overhead is the real constraint.

## Part 1 - The dissection lens

Run any offer, yours or a competitor's, through these seven layers.
Capture each one before you form an opinion. Most people skip
straight to "what's the discount" and miss the structure.

| Layer | What to capture |
|---|---|
| **Context** | Vertical, price point, AOV tier, target avatar, traffic temperature (cold ad, PDP, or email) |
| **Core offer** | What the buyer actually gets for the money, stated plainly |
| **Value stack** | Anchor price, discount mechanic, bundle, bonuses, guarantee, urgency, scarcity, payment terms, shipping |
| **Ecom mechanics** | Subscription, BOGO, free gift, shipping threshold, tiered or volume pricing, tripwire, first-order discount |
| **Why it works** | Which lever of the value equation each element is actually pulling |
| **Framing** | How the offer is worded in the ad versus on the page. These are separate levers and they are often doing different jobs. |
| **Replicability** | What transfers to another vertical versus what only works because of this specific brand's position |

That last row is the one that earns its keep. Most offer teardowns
on the internet stop at "they gave away a free gift," which tells
you nothing about whether you can do the same thing at your margin.

## Part 2 - The DTC translation layer

The high-ticket framework assumes things a physical-product business
does not have. Here is what changes.

| What the framework assumes | What DTC actually has |
|---|---|
| Near-zero marginal cost per delivery | Real COGS on every unit shipped |
| Room to be meaningfully more expensive | Price transparency across every competitor in the category |
| Bonuses that cost nothing to fulfill | Bonuses that are physical goods with their own COGS and shipping weight |
| 1-to-1 or done-for-you delivery as a value dial | A product in a box; the delivery dial barely moves |
| Refunds absorbed at 2-3x price | Refunds that cost you the unit, the shipping, and the return handling |

**What still transfers, unchanged:**

- Value is a ratio, not a number. The denominator (time and effort)
  moves it faster than the numerator.
- Proof raises perceived likelihood more cheaply than anything else
  you can do.
- Bonuses raise perceived value without touching your anchor price.
- Risk reversal is usually the single biggest conversion lever
  available to you.
- Naming the offer around the result rather than the SKU changes how
  the whole page reads.

**What has to be rebuilt for physical product:**

- Your bonus stack has a COGS line. Price it. A "free gift" that
  costs $9 to fulfill and ships in the same box is a real bonus. One
  that needs its own carton is a margin problem wearing a bonus
  costume.
- Your guarantee window has to be longer than your time-to-result,
  and your time-to-result is a physical fact you cannot compress
  with better copy. A supplement that works in six weeks needs a
  guarantee longer than six weeks, full stop.
- Your delivery dial is mostly speed and convenience. Shipping time,
  subscription cadence, and how little the customer has to think
  about reordering.

## Part 3 - The discount tension, resolved

The framework says hold price and stack value. Standard DTC practice
says run 20% off. Both camps are certain, and the argument never
resolves because they are answering different questions.

**The resolution:** treat value construction and discounting as two
separate layers.

- **Hold or raise the anchor.** The anchor is what the offer is
  measured against. Cutting it cuts everything downstream.
- **Lead with stacked bonuses and risk reversal.** These raise the
  numerator without touching the anchor.
- **Reserve real discounting for two situations only:** new-customer
  acquisition, where you are deliberately buying the first order,
  and genuine seasonal deadlines.
- **Justify the discount when you use one.** A markdown explained as
  bundle math reads as earned. The same percentage with no
  explanation reads as "this was never worth the original price."

The failure mode to avoid: a permanent sitewide discount. It trains
your list to wait, attracts your worst buyers, and quietly resets
your anchor to the discounted price, at which point you have given
away the margin and kept none of the perceived value.

## Part 4 - The money model

The offer on the page is the front end. How you acquire and monetize
a customer over their life is the money model, and it is where DTC
margin is actually won or lost.

**Front end exists to acquire, not to profit.** A deliberately cheap
entry offer is not a pricing mistake if the back end is built. Gate
it to new customers only, which both protects the loss leader and
explains to everyone else why the price is that low.

**Subscription is the retention engine.** Default to it, price the
one-time purchase higher, give the subscription permanent incentives
rather than a first-order discount, and always leave a low-friction
way to buy once. A subscription with no escape hatch converts worse
than one with it.

**AOV levers, in rough order of how well they work:**

- BOGO or buy-X-get-X reads as more generous than the mathematically
  equivalent percentage off, and moves double the units
- Tiered volume pricing with the tiers labeled, so the middle option
  has a name and a reason
- Free shipping threshold set above current AOV, with a cart
  progress indicator
- Order bumps at the decision point, complementary or aesthetic
- Per-unit or per-day price reframe, which shrinks the perceived
  number without changing it

## Part 5 - The economics gate

No offer scales on paid traffic unless the unit economics hold. Run
any proposed offer against these three before you commit spend.

- **Gross margin roughly 60-80%.** Below that, the CAC ceiling gets
  so tight that no offer construction rescues it.
- **CAC payback under 12 months.** Longer than that and you are
  financing growth out of working capital you probably do not have.
- **Lifetime value sets the CAC ceiling, not the first order.** But
  only if your retention is real and measured, not assumed.

**This is the handoff point.** If you have run your P&L to a CAC
ceiling, the number you got is the constraint every offer decision
answers to. A beautiful offer that requires paying more per customer
than your ceiling is not a good offer, it is an expensive way to
lose money faster.

If your ceiling comes back too low to acquire anyone, the offer is
not the problem. Average order value or cost structure is, and no
amount of bonus stacking fixes it.

## Part 6 - Patterns worth stealing

Drawn from teardowns of ten DTC offers across deodorant, skincare,
supplements, razors, soap, home textiles, and sleep hardware. The
pattern is the transferable part; the specific brand executions are
observations from mid-2026 and change constantly.

**Performance guarantee aimed at the single core objection.** Not
"satisfaction guaranteed," but a guarantee written against the exact
thing the buyer is afraid of. If they are afraid it will not work,
guarantee the result. If they are afraid it will be uncomfortable,
guarantee they can keep it either way.

**Social proof on the guarantee itself.** Stating how few people
claim the refund signals both that the product works and that the
generosity is not reckless.

**Progressive gift unlock by bundle tier.** Each tier adds a named
gift rather than just a bigger percentage. Converts a pricing table
into a reason to move up.

**Free-for-life gift attached to a subscription.** Cheap to fulfill,
and it makes cancelling feel like losing something.

**Branded mechanism.** Naming your process or ingredient system
gives the buyer something to believe in that competitors cannot
claim, even with an identical formula.

**Transparent discount math.** Showing the arithmetic behind a
bundle price makes the markdown read as structural rather than
arbitrary.

**Premium price held by proof rather than markdown.** The hardest
version to execute and the most durable when it works.

**Build-your-own configuration.** Letting the buyer choose scents,
variants, or components raises perceived fit and moves more units
per order than a fixed bundle.

> Verify any competitor's current price, guarantee window, or terms
> before you cite them anywhere public. These change monthly and
> stating a competitor's terms incorrectly is its own problem.

## Part 7 - Build worksheet

Answer in order. The order matters; each section constrains the next.

### A. Context

- Product:
- Avatar, niched three to four levels down:
- Price point, COGS, target margin:
- Traffic temperature this offer is written for:

### B. Value

- The specific end result the buyer wants:
- Proof you have that it will work for *them*:
- How you shrink time to first result:
- How you shrink effort and sacrifice:

### C. Core offer

- What they actually get:
- Every obstacle between them and the result, listed exhaustively:
- Each obstacle rewritten as a solution:
- The three to five components you are keeping:
- What you cut, and why:

### D. Amplifiers

- Bonus stack, each with a dollar value and the specific objection
  it kills:
- Fulfillment cost of each bonus:
- Guarantee: type, window, and the core fear it answers:
- Is the window longer than your real time-to-result?
- Scarcity or urgency, and whether the limit is genuinely real:
- Offer name, built around the result rather than the SKU:

### E. Money model

- Front end and back end:
- Subscription default, and the buy-once escape:
- Discount mechanic, and how you justify it:
- AOV levers you are deploying:

### F. Economics gate

- Gross margin on this offer as constructed:
- Blended COGS including bonus fulfillment:
- Your CAC ceiling:
- Can you acquire a customer for this offer under that ceiling?
- If not, what changes: price, cost structure, or the offer itself?

### G. The assembled offer

Write it as one paragraph, in the words it will appear on the page.
If you cannot write it as a paragraph, it is not an offer yet, it is
a list of tactics.

## What to do with this next

**Do not launch the whole thing at once.** Change one variable.
Guarantee, or bonus stack, or the tier structure. If you change four
things and conversion moves, you have learned nothing about which
one did it.

**Take the assembled paragraph to your landing page first,** before
the ads. The offer is what the page has to sell. Ads written against
an offer the page does not deliver just buy expensive bounces.

**Re-run the economics gate after launch** with real numbers rather
than projected ones. Bonus fulfillment costs and return rates are
routinely worse in practice than on paper.

**Then log what happened.** Append to `references/Log.md`:

```
## [Date] - [Offer] - [Brief label]
**What happened:** [what you changed and what it did]
**Impact:** [conversion, AOV, margin, return rate]
**Watch for:** [what to do differently next time]
```

Three offers in, that file is worth more than this document, because
it is about your category and your buyers rather than DTC in
general.

## When you want a second set of eyes

The hardest part of offer work is not construction, it is judgement.
Which objection is actually costing you the sale. Whether your
category will bear a premium price or punish it. Whether the
guarantee that reads as generous is one you can afford at your
return rate.

This document gives you the structure. It cannot make those calls
for you, and getting them wrong is expensive in a way that only
shows up two months later.

willing to do it without checking whether the economics survive. The
